If you want to train as a doctor abroad, start with a realistic picture. Clinical medicine takes five or six years, often includes a language requirement for work with patients, and is left out of many scholarships: the Romanian Government Scholarship, for example, does not offer medicine, dentistry or pharmacy at all.

There are exceptions. Czech public universities, including Charles University, teach every degree free in Czech, whatever your nationality, so a medical degree costs you a preparatory language year and your living costs. Türkiye Bursları funds medicine in full, with a Turkish language year included, but sets a minimum grade of 90% for medical applicants.

The picture is much brighter after a first degree. Public health, global health and health sciences master's degrees fit the development goals of the big government awards, and Helsinki's tuition waivers apply to its English-taught master's, health programmes among them. At doctoral level, medical and life-science research is funded like the other sciences: salaried PhD posts in Norway and Sweden, and federal awards such as Canada's doctoral scholarship.

Whatever you choose, read the eligibility rules for clinical work carefully. A degree that lets you practise in one country does not always let you practise at home, so check with your own medical council before you commit years to a programme.
