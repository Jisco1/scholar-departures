Whether it is called a motivation letter, a statement of purpose, a personal statement or a set of essays, this document is where most funded applications are won or lost. Grades get you past the first filter. The letter decides whether a committee — who may read two hundred of them in a week — remembers you.

This guide is about writing it yourself, well. Many institutions now set rules on the use of AI tools in application documents, and some ban them outright; read each programme's policy before you start.

What committees are actually looking for

Every funder is asking the same underlying question: if we invest in this person, what will happen that would not happen otherwise? A strong letter answers it with three things:

  1. Evidence of ability — what you have already done, not what you promise to do.
  2. A specific plan — what you will study, why this programme, and what you will do afterwards.
  3. Fit with the funder's purpose — Chevening looks for leaders, the Commonwealth scholarships for development impact, Gates Cambridge for commitment to improving others' lives, DAAD's EPOS courses for professional experience in development.

A structure that works

1. Open with a concrete moment, not a quotation

Skip "Since I was a child I have been passionate about…". Start with a specific problem you saw or worked on. "In 2024 our district clinic ran out of insulin twice because nobody could predict demand." One sentence like that says more about your motivation than a paragraph of adjectives.

2. Show what you have done about it

Your degree, projects, jobs and volunteering — chosen and ordered to support your plan, not listed like a CV. For each, say what you did and what changed because of it. Numbers help: people trained, costs cut, results published.

3. Explain the gap

What can't you do yet? This is the bridge to the programme: the skill, method or network you are missing, stated honestly.

4. Why this programme, specifically

Name modules, research groups, labs or professors, and connect each to your gap. If a sentence would be equally true of any university, cut it. Committees can tell immediately when a letter has been sent to twenty places with the name changed.

5. The plan after graduation

Be concrete about the first two to five years: the role, the organisation or type of organisation, and the problem you will work on. For awards with a return commitment — Chevening, the Commonwealth scholarships, many Mastercard Foundation programmes — show how your plan works in your home country.

6. Close by connecting back

Return to the opening problem and show how the funding closes the loop. Two or three sentences are enough.

Common mistakes

  • Generic praise of the university's ranking or "world-class facilities". The committee knows.
  • A life story without a point. Every paragraph should support your plan.
  • Ignoring the word or character limit. Online forms often cut off text silently.
  • Hardship as the whole story. Difficult circumstances can be powerful context, but the letter must still show what you did and what you will do.
  • Unexplained gaps or weak grades. Address them briefly and factually if they are obvious on your transcript.
  • Copying phrases from online samples. Some institutions check for similarity, and borrowed sentences read as borrowed.

Editing checklist

  1. Does the first paragraph make a reader want to continue?
  2. Could any paragraph be moved to another applicant's letter unchanged? Rewrite it.
  3. Is every claim backed by an example?
  4. Does it answer exactly the prompt this funder asked — not the one from your last application?
  5. Have you read it aloud? Awkward sentences reveal themselves when spoken.
  6. Has one critical reader — not a friend who will only praise it — read it and told you what they remember?
Length guide: DAAD asks for one to three pages; Chevening uses four essays with character limits; many European master's programmes want one to two pages; US colleges set strict word limits. When no limit is given, one to two pages is safe.
