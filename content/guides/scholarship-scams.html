Fully funded scholarships attract exactly the people scammers look for: hopeful, short on money, and racing a deadline. The good news is that scams follow a small number of patterns. Learn them once and you will recognise almost every fake offer in seconds.

The one rule that catches most scams: a real scholarship pays you. If anyone asks you to pay to receive, "process", "secure" or "release" a scholarship, stop.

The seven warning signs

1. You have to pay to receive the award

"Processing fees", "release fees", "insurance deposits" and "tax clearance" payments are the core of most scholarship fraud. Genuine awards are paid to you or straight to the university. Be careful not to confuse this with an admission application fee: some universities, especially in the USA, charge a fee to apply for a place — but it is paid on the university's own application system, it is the same for everyone, and many schools will waive it (see our guide on US financial aid).

2. You "won" something you never applied for

Scholarship providers do not pick strangers from the internet. An email or message saying you have been "selected" or "shortlisted" for an award you did not apply to is a scam, however official the logo looks.

3. The address is not the institution's own

Real universities and scholarship bodies write from their own domain — the part after the @ in an email, and the address at the top of your browser. Watch for free email accounts (a "DAAD officer" writing from a Gmail address), and for look-alike domains that add a word, swap a letter or use a different ending. On every route page here, the official domain is printed on the button so you can compare.

4. Guarantees

No honest agent, website or consultant can guarantee a scholarship, a visa or admission. Competitive awards are decided by selection committees, and visas by governments. A guarantee is a sales tactic.

5. Pressure and secrecy

"Reply within 24 hours", "only three places left", "don't discuss this offer with anyone" — urgency and secrecy are designed to stop you checking. Real deadlines are published on the official website months in advance.

6. Requests for passwords, bank logins or ID scans up front

Nobody legitimate needs your email password or online-banking login. Copies of your passport and certificates are normally uploaded only through the institution's own application portal, not sent to an individual over chat or email.

7. A package deal: scholarship + visa + job

Offers that bundle a scholarship with a guaranteed visa, a job and accommodation — for a fee — target people who want to migrate as much as to study. They are almost always fraudulent or wildly overpriced.

Scams that copy real processes

The most convincing scams imitate a step that genuinely exists:

  • Fake blocked-account providers. Students going to Germany usually must show proof of funds, often through a blocked account. The money in a real blocked account stays yours and is released to you monthly. Use only a provider that appears on the German Federal Foreign Office's own information pages, and never send the deposit to a private person or a provider you found through a message.
  • Impersonated social media pages. Fake pages named after well-known awards — Chevening, DAAD, Mastercard Foundation, Fulbright — collect "application fees" or personal data. Reach programmes by typing their official address yourself, or through the links on our route pages.
  • "Agents" selling access. Many education agents are legitimate and are paid by universities. But no agent can sell you a government scholarship: Chevening, Commonwealth, DAAD, Fulbright, Stipendium Hungaricum and Türkiye Bursları are applied for directly and are free to apply for.
  • Fake admission letters. A letter that arrives without you ever submitting an application, or that asks for a "deposit" before you can see it, is not an admission letter.

Five checks before you trust any offer

  1. Find the programme yourself. Type the university or provider's address into your browser, or use the official links on our route pages. Do not click the link in the message.
  2. Match the details. Is the award described the same way on the official site? Are the dates, amounts and eligibility the same?
  3. Check the sender's domain against the official website's domain, letter by letter.
  4. Write to the official contact listed on the institution's website — not the one in the message — and ask whether the offer is genuine.
  5. Search the exact wording of the offer together with the word "scam". Fraudsters reuse their scripts.

What genuinely costs money

Some costs in a real application are normal. Knowing them makes the fake ones stand out:

Normal costPaid to
Admission application fee (some universities; waivers are common)The university, on its own application system
English test such as IELTS, TOEFL or Duolingo English TestThe test provider
Certified copies and translations of documentsA notary, sworn translator or the issuing institution
Visa application feeThe embassy or its official visa centre
Proof-of-funds deposit (for example a German blocked account)A regulated provider — the money remains yours

Notice what is missing: there is no legitimate fee for being awarded a scholarship.

If you have already paid or shared details

  • Stop replying and do not send anything else, even if they promise a refund for one more payment.
  • Contact your bank or mobile-money provider immediately; some payments can be stopped or reversed if you act quickly.
  • Change any password you shared and turn on two-step verification.
  • Report it to the institution being impersonated, to the platform where you were contacted, and to your national police cybercrime unit.
  • Keep screenshots, email headers and payment receipts as evidence.

Being targeted is not a sign of carelessness — these schemes are run professionally. Telling friends what happened is one of the best ways to stop the same script reaching them.
