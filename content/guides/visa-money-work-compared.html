Three questions decide whether a study-abroad plan works financially, whatever your nationality: how much money you must prove before you get a student visa, how much you can earn while you study, and how long you can stay afterwards to find a job. The answers differ a lot from country to country, and most of them change every year.

The tables below compare ten popular destinations. Every figure was checked on 5 October 2026 against the government or university page listed under Sources. Each country also has its own guide with more detail.

Before you rely on a number: check it again on the official page when you apply. The Netherlands updates its amount every 1 January, Canada raised its figure on 1 September 2026, and Germany's follows its student grant rates.

Proof of funds for a student visa

This is the money you must show you have, usually for the first year, before a visa or residence permit is issued. It is separate from tuition, which most countries also expect to see paid or covered.

How to meet the requirement

  • A full scholarship usually counts. An award letter showing a stipend at or above the required amount is accepted in place of your own money by most countries in this table. Keep the letter with your visa documents.
  • Blocked accounts: Germany and Belgium in particular expect the money to be locked in an account and released monthly. Use only a provider your university or the embassy names, and read our scam guide first.
  • Sponsors: a parent or sponsor can often support you, but usually with their own proof of income and a formal undertaking, and some consulates accept money only from close relatives.

Staying after graduation to find a job

A post-study permit gives you time to find a job after you graduate, and in most countries you may work while you look. It is often the deciding factor for students who hope to start a career abroad.

What this means for choosing a country

  • Lowest proof of funds: Austria for students under 24 (about €8,700 for a year), then Ireland (€10,000) and Italy (about €10,200). Switzerland asks for the most: CHF 21,000 at ETH Zürich.
  • Most generous work rules during study: Canada's 24 hours a week, and Ireland's 40 hours in the holidays.
  • Longest time to find a job: Canada (up to three years), the UK after a PhD (three years), Ireland after a master's or PhD (up to two years) and Germany (18 months). Switzerland gives only six months.
  • Scholarship holders should read their award terms: some, like Chevening and Commonwealth, require you to go home, whatever the country allows.
