Two listings can both say "free" and leave you with bills that differ by thousands of euros a year. The difference is in the label. On this site every route carries one or more tags — Tuition-Free, Full Scholarship, Need-Based Aid, Fee Waiver, Merit and Low Tuition — and each means something specific. This guide explains them, so you can compare routes honestly and spot the gaps before they become debts.

The one question to ask of any award: after this, who pays for tuition, rent, food, insurance, the visa and the flight? If the answer to any of these is "you", budget for it now.

Tuition-Free

A tuition-free route is a public university that charges international students no tuition. Germany's public universities are the best-known example: RWTH Aachen, Freie Universität Berlin and Universität Hamburg charge no tuition to any nationality.

What it covers: the teaching. What it does not cover: almost everything else. You still pay a semester contribution (about €350–€400 a semester at the German universities on this board), your rent, food and health insurance — and for a German student visa you normally have to prove €11,904 a year before you travel.

Tuition-free is also sometimes conditional. The Czech Republic is free only for degrees taught in Czech; Norway ended free tuition for non-EU/EEA students in 2023; Iceland's public universities will charge non-EEA bachelor's and master's students from autumn 2027. Our guide to the real cost of free tuition goes country by country.

Full Scholarship

A full scholarship pays tuition and a living allowance, and usually travel, insurance and visa costs as well. Chevening, the Commonwealth Master's Scholarships, Gates Cambridge, Rhodes, Erasmus Mundus, Türkiye Scholarships and the Mastercard Foundation programmes all fit here.

Even "full" awards differ. Compare the monthly allowance with the real cost of living in the city: Stipendium Hungaricum pays bachelor's and master's students HUF 43,700 a month (about €110) plus free housing — explicitly "a contribution" to living costs — while Gates Cambridge pays £22,050 a year. Both are full scholarships; only one will cover a comfortable life on its own.

Some full scholarships are only full for part of a degree. Ghent University's doctoral scholarships fund 24 months in Belgium but require a home institution to fund the other 24; Ireland's GOI-IES award funds one year of a longer PhD.

Need-Based Aid

Need-based aid is money calculated from your family's finances. The strongest version — found at Harvard, MIT, Yale, Princeton, Amherst, Dartmouth and Brown — combines two promises:

  • Need-blind admission: the college decides whether to admit you without considering whether you can pay.
  • Meeting full need: once you are admitted, the college gives enough grant aid to cover what your family cannot.

For a student from a low-income family this can mean a completely free degree, including housing, food and travel. For a better-off family it may mean a partial discount. The only way to know is to run the college's net price calculator with your real figures. Our guide to US financial aid for international students explains the forms and the terms.

"Need-aware" colleges, such as Duke and Colby, meet full need for the international students they admit but do consider need when deciding whom to admit.

Fee Waiver

A fee waiver removes the application fee — the cost of applying, not the cost of studying. Colby charges no application fee at all; Harvard waives it if it would be a hardship; the Mastercard Foundation programme at Cambridge waives it for eligible applicants. Fee waivers matter because application fees across a list of eight or ten US colleges can reach several hundred dollars.

Merit

Merit awards are won on academic or leadership strength rather than financial need. They range from full rides — Vanderbilt's Cornelius Vanderbilt Scholarship pays full tuition, ETH Zürich's ESOP pays living costs and waives tuition — to partial discounts, such as the 50% tuition waivers that make up most of the University of Helsinki's awards. The tag tells you the money is competitive; the route page tells you how much it is.

Low Tuition

A low-tuition route charges international students fees that are small by international standards. Austrian public universities charge non-EU students €726.72 a semester — and nothing at all to citizens of the least developed countries. Low-tuition routes are listed because, combined with savings or a smaller scholarship, they can make a European degree affordable.

How to compare two routes fairly

  1. Add up the full yearly cost: tuition, fees, rent, food, insurance, visa, travel.
  2. Subtract what the award actually pays, using the figures on the official page.
  3. Check the length: does the funding last for the whole degree, and what are the conditions for renewal (for example, earning a minimum number of credits)?
  4. Check the strings: return-home requirements, work restrictions, age limits and nationality lists.
  5. Check the date: every route page shows when it was last verified. Programmes change — the official page has the final word.

Done honestly, this turns a list of "free" offers into a short list of routes you can actually afford — which is the whole point.
