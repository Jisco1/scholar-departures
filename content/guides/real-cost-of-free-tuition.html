"Study in Europe for free" is one of the most-shared study-abroad claims on the internet. Parts of it are true. But the claim hides three things: the fees that remain when tuition is zero, the money you must prove before you get a visa, and the countries where "free" quietly stopped applying to international students. Here is the fuller picture, using figures checked against official sources in September 2026.

Where "free" has ended for international students

  • Norway: public universities have charged non-EU/EEA students tuition since 2023. Salaried PhD fellowships remain a funded route.
  • Iceland: from autumn 2027, public universities will charge non-EEA bachelor's and master's students tuition. Doctoral programmes are exempt.
  • Parts of Germany: the state of Baden-Württemberg charges non-EU students €1,500 a semester, and TU Munich has charged non-EU students since winter 2024.
  • Finland and Sweden: English-taught degrees charge non-EU students tuition — Helsinki's master's fees are €13,000–€18,000 a year — although scholarships can cover it.

The semester contribution

In Germany and several other countries, every student pays a contribution each semester to fund student services, the students' union and often a public-transport pass. It is not tuition, and it is usually a few hundred euros. At RWTH Aachen it is about €350, most of which is the Deutschlandsemesterticket for regional transport across Germany. Pay it on time: it is usually a condition of enrolment.

Proof of funds: the cost before you arrive

Before issuing a student visa, most countries want evidence that you can support yourself. This is often the biggest hurdle for students on a tuition-free route, because the money must exist before you travel.

  • Germany: €992 a month, or €11,904 a year, usually shown with a blocked account from which €992 is released each month.
  • Ireland: at least €10,000 a year, plus at least €6,000 of tuition paid before the visa is granted.
  • United Kingdom: £1,529 a month in London or £1,171 elsewhere, for up to nine months, plus the Immigration Health Surcharge of £776 a year.
  • Canada: CAD 23,448 for a single applicant's living costs from 1 September 2026, on top of tuition and travel.

A scholarship award letter often counts as proof of funds — one more reason a stipend can matter as much as a tuition waiver.

Living costs

Rent is the largest cost in most university cities, and student housing is scarce in many of them — Amsterdam, Delft, Munich, Dublin and Zürich especially. Budget for health insurance (compulsory in Germany, Switzerland and the Netherlands), a mobile phone, books, and a deposit of one to three months' rent.

Turning free tuition into a free degree

The routes that cover living costs are what turn "free tuition" into a free degree:

  • In Germany, a DAAD scholarship of €992 a month on top of zero tuition.
  • In Italy, a regional DSU grant of up to about €7,000 a year plus a fee exemption.
  • In Austria, the least-developed-country tuition exemption combined with savings or a scholarship.
  • Across Europe, Erasmus Mundus at €1,400 a month, or Stipendium Hungaricum and Türkiye Scholarships with housing included.

Free tuition is a real advantage. Treat it as the start of your funding plan, not the end of it.
